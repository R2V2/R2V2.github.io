Negation

How do we learn what "not" means? It's difficult (if not impossible) to point to in the world, yet at around 2 and a half years, children are able to use truth-functional negation to perform fairly complex tasks. There has been much debate as to when children gain this ability in comprehension,See work by Feiman and de Carvalho (not yet published). but not much work has focused on how they do so given the unique challenges negation (and other logical terms) present. Past work has suggested a typology of negation whereby negative elements can mean prohibition ("No running!"), nonexistence ("No more water"), and denial or truth-functional negation ("That's not a dog") among others. This research also found that children produce truth-functional negation later than other kinds of negation and later work put forth supporting evidence for comprehension. This may be because, as some have proposed, they lack the conceptual machinery to represent such an operation. Or, it may be beause, relative to the other kinds of negation, truth-functional negation contains fewer perceptual cues (there's nothing disappearing, or an angry tone letting you know it's time to stop), and may be learned by integrating both perceptual and linguistic information. In other words, I am interested in whether negation as a concept must be learned. If so, then how is it learned and what changes throughout development? If not, then how do we map it onto language? I'm also interested in how negation is processed in real-time and how it relates to inhibitory control.

Symmetry

For most words, there is an assymetryical or predicative relationship of word order. By this I mean that, in "brown dog," we predicate "brownness" to "dog" and not the other way around. The case is perhaps clearest with verbs, since we know that there's an important difference between "I saw the guard," and "The guard saw me." For some words, however, this doesn't seem to be the case. In most cases for these words, the "inverse" order is entailed as well. For example, if I'm equal to you, then it's necessarily true that you're equal to me. We call these words which strongly entail the inverse ordering "symmetricals."

A relation R is symmetrical if and only if (iff) for all x,y: if R(x,y), then R(y,x).

Gleitman et al. 2019, The emergence of the formal category “symmetry”in a new sign language"

Symmetricals are often verbs, but they can also be nouns (e.g., "cousin," "brother"), prepositions (e.g., "near," "by"), and adjectives (e.g., "similar," "equal"). Unlike other "non-symmetrical verbs," they seem to always require multiple participants (e.g., "John kicks" works but "John meets" doesn't). I'm interested in how we learn that some words are symmetrical while others are not, and what this says about the structure of language.
